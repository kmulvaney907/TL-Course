Read This First

USE AND LIMITATIONS

Not an NTOA, CATO, or California POST course; not a credential, authorization, operational order, legal opinion, or substitute for agency policy. All real operations, tactics, delegation and force decisions remain governed by current APD policy, applicable law and the designated chain of command. This course teaches leadership reasoning and risk management, not technical assault, breaching, sniper, or weapons procedures. Validate local policies with command and legal counsel. Do not place nonpublic incident details into unsecured services.

WHAT COMPLETION MEANS

Completion of this course means academic preparation is complete. It does not mean the learner is qualified, authorized or ready to lead tactical operations. Verbal briefing, live coordination, decisions under time pressure and AAR facilitation cannot be validated by self-study. Command retains sole qualification authority.

Course outcomes

At the end of the course the learner can do the following in writing, using the worksheets:

  1. Distinguish the ATL, TL, tactical commander and incident commander functions and state the limits of delegated authority that apply to the learner.

  2. Frame a tactical problem: separate facts from assumptions, state the safety objective, and identify risk, constraints and critical unknowns.

  3. Produce a strategic-level briefing and a risk register for a planned operation.

  4. Write clear assignments, situation updates and a complete transfer-of-command briefing.

Role, Doctrine and Delegated Authority

Why this matters

A new ATL or TL stops being judged only on personal performance. The leader is now responsible for what the team understands, what the commander knows and whether each decision is made by the person with the authority to make it. Unclear authority causes two opposite failures: a leader who acts beyond authority, and a leader who waits for permission that was already given.

Lesson

1. Four functions

The descriptions below are original course material. They describe functions, not ranks. Replace them with APD policy language in the local annex when you have it.

  • Incident commander. Sets the incident objectives and priorities and integrates all agencies and resources at the incident.

  • Tactical commander. Directs the tactical capability assigned to the incident, within the authority the incident commander and policy give.

  • Team leader. Turns the commander’s objectives into a team-level plan, assigns and controls team personnel, and reports changes.

  • Assistant team leader. Supports the TL: tracks assignments and information, coordinates with supporting units, prepares to take over the TL function, and makes the decisions that have been delegated.

Worked example

During a fictional barricade, the incident commander sets the objective “protect the public and resolve without injury”. The tactical commander decides that the team will hold a perimeter position while CNT attempts contact. The TL assigns positions and a report schedule. The ATL keeps the assignment log, confirms that the CNT liaison has the team’s radio channel, and tells the TL that two operators have been in place for three hours. None of the four has done another’s job.

Common error

Treating rank as the function. A senior sergeant who arrives is not in command of anything until command is transferred and announced (Module 4).

2. Decision rights

Every decision a tactical leader can face belongs in one of four categories:

Category Meaning
A. ATL may decide Inside standing delegated authority. Decide, then inform the TL.
B. TL approval Recommend to the TL. Act when approved.
C. Commander approval TL or ATL recommends through the chain to the tactical or incident commander.
D. Emergent safety action Immediate action to protect life when delay would cause harm, followed by immediate notification. Policy and law define when this applies.

Write the scope and the conditions of every delegation. “You handle logistics” is not a delegation. “You may reassign operators between perimeter positions for relief without asking me, and you tell me within five minutes” is one.

Worked example

Worksheet 7 in Part III shows five illustrative rows. They are examples of form only and are not APD policy. In the example, “request an additional ambulance to staging” sits in category A, and “change the team’s mission from containment to arrest” sits in category C.

Common error

Placing a decision in category D because it feels urgent. Urgency of feeling is not the test. The test is the one APD policy and law set for emergent action. If you cannot state that test from policy, mark the row “unconfirmed” and ask.

3. Doctrine, strategy, tactic

  • Doctrine is a principle that holds across incidents. Example: “Decisions follow the safety priorities.”

  • Strategy is the approach chosen for this incident. Example: “Contain and negotiate.”

  • Tactic is a specific method. Example: a particular way to deliver a phone to a subject.

These three definitions are original course wording. Charles “Sid” Heal’s Sound Doctrine (Part VI, enrichment) treats the subject at length.

Why the difference matters

Skill with a tactic is not a reason to use it. The question runs downward: what principle applies, what approach serves the objective, and only then which method.

Common error

Starting the discussion with the method (“we can do X”) before the objective and the authority are stated.

4. The leadership shift

An operator attends to the task in front of him. A leader must also attend to the system around the task. Use this list as a scan:

  1. Does each subordinate understand the objective and the limit?

  2. Which information is verified, and which is not?

  3. Who outside the team is exposed?

  4. Are communications working, and has each key message been acknowledged?

  5. Is medical support in place and briefed?

  6. What does delay cost, and what does action cost?

  7. What is the next decision point, and who owns it?

5. Challenge culture

A subordinate who points out a missing fact, an unsupported assumption or a policy conflict is supporting command. The leader’s part is to ask for the challenge before the decision (“What am I missing?”), listen, and then decide within authority. The subordinate’s part is to state the concern once, clearly, and then carry out a lawful decision.

Worked example

ATL to TL: “Before we brief: the occupancy information is from yesterday afternoon. We have nothing from today. I recommend we mark it unverified in the brief.” This is a challenge to information, stated with a recommendation, at a time when it can be used.

Check yourself

Write your answers, then see Part IX.

  1. A lieutenant from another division arrives at your scene and begins to give your operators instructions. Command has not been transferred. In two sentences, state the problem and what you do.

    Keyed answer

    Problem: two sources of direction with no transfer of command; operators cannot tell whose direction controls. Action: tell the lieutenant who is in command and how to reach them; tell your TL at once. You do not argue rank and you do not let operators receive conflicting direction. Misconception a wrong answer suggests: that rank equals command.

  2. Classify each as doctrine, strategy or tactic: (a) “Hold and negotiate while the perimeter is secure.” (b) “Hostages and victims come first in the safety priorities.” (c) “Use the armored vehicle’s public address system to make announcements.”

    Keyed answer

    Answers: (a) Strategy. (b) Doctrine. (c) Tactic.

  3. Your TL says, “You handle the support units tonight.” State two questions you ask so that this becomes a real delegation.

    Keyed answer

    Any two of: What exactly may I decide without asking you? Under what conditions? What do you want reported, and how soon? What is outside it? Why: a delegation needs scope and conditions.

Decision-Making, Bias and Risk

Why this matters

Most poor tactical decisions are not caused by a lack of skill. They come from a wrong picture of the situation: a label accepted as a fact, a first report never rechecked, pressure to act. A decision model does not make the decision. It makes sure the right questions are asked, in order, every time, including when the leader is tired and under stress.

Lesson

1. Frame the problem

A dispatch label (“hostage situation”, “barricade”) is a claim. Before you accept it, sort what you have into three lists.

  • Facts: each with its source and time.

  • Inferences: what you conclude from the facts.

  • Unknowns: what you need and do not have.

Then write the working theory in one sentence and ask: what would I expect to see if this theory is wrong?

Then state the safety objective and the decision required now. “End the incident” is a wish. “Decide whether to recommend evacuation of the adjoining unit” is a decision.

Worked example

See the completed decision record in Part III (Example 1). It sorts a short fictional report into the three lists and names the disproving evidence.

Common error

Writing an inference in the facts list. “Subject is armed” is a fact only if someone identified saw a weapon at a stated time. Otherwise write: “Caller reported a handgun at 2140; not confirmed.”

2. Safety priorities

TROS (p. 11, Figure A-1) lists four safety priorities in order: hostages and victims; innocent bystanders and the public; public safety personnel, including SWAT and EMS; and the hostage taker or suspect. TROS states that the order reflects how much jeopardy each group is in and how much ability each has to control the outcome.

Course commentary, not a quotation from TROS: use the priorities to decide whose risk a decision must reduce first. The order does not say one life is worth more than another, and it does not authorize force. Force is authorized only by law and APD policy.

Worked example

A subject is alone in a house with no one else exposed and a secure perimeter. The people at greatest risk from a hasty entry are the officers and the subject. The priorities do not point toward urgency. Add a credible report of an injured person inside, and the first priority is now engaged; the analysis changes.

3. Decision models

A model is a checklist of questions. Use one consistently. Three are common.

PERF Critical Decision-Making Model. Five steps, repeated as information changes (PERF 2023, p. 12):

  1. Gather information (PERF’s training modules say “collect information”).

  2. Assess the situation, threats and risks.

  3. Consider police powers and agency policy.

  4. Identify options and determine the best course of action.

  5. Act, review and reassess.

PERF designed ICAT mainly for persons in crisis who are unarmed or armed with something other than a firearm (PERF 2023, p. 4). The five steps are general, but the surrounding ICAT training has that scope.

OODA. Observe, orient, decide, act, and repeat. The model is attributed to Col. John Boyd, USAF. It is a description of a cycle, not a procedure. Its practical lesson for a team leader: orientation (what you believe the situation is) drives everything after it, so test your orientation when new observations do not fit. NTOA’s Team Leader Development course includes a block on the OODA loop.

PIETO. A question set: Priorities, Intelligence, Environment, Tools/Tactics, Officer instincts. A 2019 Police1 article attributes it to Lt. Dan Murphy of Fort Collins Police Services. It is a practitioner’s model, not an official NTOA standard, and it does not appear in the TROS pages checked for this course.

No model is a source of legal authority. Step 3 of the CDM exists because the model itself authorizes nothing.

Common error

Reciting the model after the decision to justify it. The model is used before the decision, and the record shows it.

4. How stress and bias distort the picture

Stress. A review of 20 studies of police and military officers found that memory for high-stress incidents was less detailed and less accurate than for low-stress incidents, and that information at the edge of attention suffered more than information about the main threat (Di Nota et al., 2021). The same review describes stress as narrowing attention onto the perceived threat and distorting the sense of distance and time.

What this means for a leader:

  • An officer at the point of contact may not have seen or heard what happened at the edges. Do not treat one account as complete.

  • You are subject to the same effect. Under pressure your attention narrows to the problem in front of you. Give someone else the job of watching the rest (Module 4).

  • Confidence is not accuracy. The review reports that talking with colleagues raised confidence but not accuracy.

Three biases. The descriptions below come from the general research literature on judgment (for example, Tversky and Kahneman, 1974, on anchoring; Baron and Hershey, 1988, on outcome bias).

Bias What it looks like on scene Control
Anchoring The first report said “hostage”. Three later reports do not support it, but the plan is still built on it. At each update ask: if this were the first thing I heard, what would I think? Keep the facts list dated.
Action bias Nothing has happened for two hours. Someone says, “We have to do something.” Ask what time is doing. Is risk rising, stable or falling? Write the trigger that would justify a change.
Outcome bias “Nobody got hurt, so it was a good plan.” In review, judge the decision by what was known then. Module 7 covers this.
Worked example of anchoring

A neighbor reports a second person inside. The team plans for a hostage. An hour later: the caller left before seeing anyone; the subject’s car has one set of tracks in the snow; a relative says the subject lives alone. A leader anchored on “hostage” explains each fact away. A leader using the dated facts list writes: “Second person: one unverified report at 1905, three items against. Classification: barricade, possible second person not excluded.”

5. Threat, risk and time

Use five words precisely.

  • Likelihood: how probable the harmful event is, on the evidence.

  • Consequence: how bad the harm would be.

  • Vulnerability: who is exposed to it.

  • Uncertainty: how much of the estimate rests on unverified information.

  • Reversibility: whether the action can be undone if it proves wrong.

Then ask what time is doing. Time may be working for safety (the subject is tiring, negotiators have contact, resources are arriving) or against it (a victim is injured, the weather is worsening, the subject is escalating). A stable scene is not automatically a safe one, and a quiet one is not automatically improving.

A good decision trigger has three properties. It is specific (names the event), observable (someone can see or hear it) and communicable (can be said in one radio transmission).

  • Weak: “If things get worse.”

  • Strong: “If the subject leaves the structure toward the east yard while the neighbor’s house is still occupied, the perimeter team notifies me at once and I recommend to the TL that we …”

The sentence ends with a recommendation because the response must match your authority (Module 1).

6. The decision record

Record at the time: the time; information known and its source; alternatives considered; the policy and authority check; the option chosen and why; what you communicated and to whom; and the trigger for reassessment. Never rewrite the record after the result is known. A record that shows a reasonable decision on incomplete information is a good record.

Check yourself

  1. Sort into fact, inference or unknown: (a) “Dispatch: caller states her brother has a rifle; she has not seen it today.” (b) “He’s obviously suicidal.” (c) “Patrol confirms the back door is open; observed 2212.”

    Keyed answer

    Answers: (a) Fact that the caller made the statement; the rifle’s presence today is unknown. (b) Inference. (c) Fact, with source and time.

  2. A scene has been quiet for 90 minutes. A supervisor says the team should “push the issue”. Write two questions you ask before you respond.

    Keyed answer

    Acceptable: What has changed in the risk? Is time working for or against safety? What trigger did we set, and has it occurred? What would the proposed action reduce? Bias at risk: action bias.

  3. Rewrite this trigger so that it is specific, observable and communicable: “We go if he becomes a threat.”

    Keyed answer

    Example: “If the subject comes out of the front door holding the rifle, Position 1 reports it at once on TAC-1, and the TL decides the response.” Any answer that names an observable event, a reporter and a decision owner is acceptable. “Becomes a threat” is a conclusion, not an observation.

Initial brief. Facts: a text at about 1500 (sister); he owns a rifle, not seen for a year (sister); truck present, no answer, no sound (patrol, 1638). Lesson

1. Mission analysis

Answer five questions in writing.

  1. Task. What has the team been asked to do?

  2. Purpose. Why? What does the requesting unit need?

  3. Authority. What legal process and what command approval exist? What do they permit?

  4. Safety priorities. Who is exposed, in what order of jeopardy?

  5. End state. What observable conditions show success?

Separate three things that are often mixed: the essential objective (the suspect is in custody), assumptions (he will be at the address), and desirable outcomes (evidence is recovered at the same time). A plan that takes on extra risk for a desirable outcome needs a deliberate decision by the person with authority. A mission that grows during planning without that decision is mission creep.

Worked example

Request: “Detectives want SWAT to arrest a robbery suspect at his girlfriend’s apartment Thursday.” Mission analysis: Task — take one named adult into custody on a felony arrest warrant. Purpose — the investigators assess the arrest as high risk and outside patrol capability. Authority — arrest warrant confirmed and attached; entry authority for a third party’s residence is a legal question for the case detective and counsel. End state — suspect in custody, no injuries, residence secured and released to the investigators. Note what the analysis found: an open legal question that must be answered before any plan is approved.

Common error

An end state that describes activity (“warrant served”) instead of conditions (“suspect in custody; occupants accounted for; no injuries”).

2. Threat assessment

For each item of threat information record the source, the date and your confidence.

Information Source Date Confidence
Suspect used a handgun in the charged offense Charging document 3 weeks ago High
Firearms are accessible at the address Unnamed associate, through detective Unknown Low: single source, undated
Two adults live at the address Surveillance 9 days ago Medium: dated

Then list the critical unknowns: the facts that would change the plan if you had them. Give each to someone to resolve, with a deadline.

Two rules. First, old information is marked old. Surveillance from nine days ago describes nine days ago. Second, possibility is not capability. “He could have a rifle” is true of everyone. The assessment records evidence.

3. Courses of action

Develop at least two approaches that are lawful and feasible, at the strategic level. Compare them against the same six criteria:

  1. Does it achieve the end state?

  2. Who is exposed, and for how long?

  3. How much time does it need, and is that time available?

  4. How much depends on unverified information?

  5. How heavy is the coordination burden?

  6. What happens if it fails, and can it be reversed?

Reject any option that cannot meet authorization or safety requirements. Record the rejected option and the reason. The record of the alternative is what shows the decision was a decision.

Worked example

Example 2 in Part III compares “arrest at the residence” with “arrest away from the residence” at this level and shows the comparison table. It does not select tactics.

Common error

A second COA that is the first one with small changes. If both options fail on the same unverified fact, you have one option.

4. Briefing order

Brief in the same order every time, so that listeners know where each item will come:

  1. Situation.

  2. Verified and unverified information.

  3. Mission, purpose and end state.

  4. Authority and constraints.

  5. Assignments and who supervises whom.

  6. Communications and reporting.

  7. Medical and logistics.

  8. Contingencies, triggers and who owns each.

  9. Questions and read-back.

The read-back is the test. Ask two people to state the end state and their own trigger. If they cannot, the brief failed, whatever its content.

5. Red-team questions

Before approval, ask:

  • What must be true for this plan to work?

  • What happens if each assumption is wrong?

  • Which element or person is overloaded?

  • Who can stop the operation, and how do they say so?

  • Does another approach meet the objective with less exposure?

6. The risk register

A risk register is a table with one row per risk. Each row has: the risk; likelihood; consequence; the control that reduces it; the owner; and the trigger that shows the risk is occurring. A risk with no owner is not controlled. Worksheet 6 is the blank form. Example 3 is completed.

7. Cold-weather overlay

For Anchorage operations, add these to the register as risks, not as comfort items: hours of daylight; ice and movement time; exposure time for personnel in static positions; battery and equipment performance in cold; road and access closures; and transport time to medical care.

  2. Your only information on occupancy is surveillance from 12 days ago. Where does it go in the brief, and what do you do about it?

  3. A detective supervisor asks you to move the operation up by two days and gives no reason. Name two things you need before you can recommend yes or no.

Lesson

1. Common operating picture

Keep one written record, visible to those who need it, with: incident objectives; current hazards; verified changes with times; assignments; resources on scene and requested; contacts; and open critical questions. The ATL normally maintains it. Its test: if the TL were removed from the scene now, could the next leader continue from the record alone?

2. Transfer of command

FEMA IS-200.c teaches that a transfer-of-command briefing covers the situation status, incident objectives and priorities, the current organization, resource information and the communications plan, among other items. This course uses that list and adds four items that tactical incidents need. The additions are course practice, not FEMA text.

A complete transfer briefing covers:

  1. Incident history and current situation.

  2. Objectives and priorities.

  3. Threat and risk, including what is unverified.

  4. Current plan and assignments.

  5. Resources: on scene, requested, released.

  6. Communications plan.

  7. Constraints and restrictions (course addition).

  8. Pending decisions and approvals (course addition).

  9. Delegated authority in effect (course addition).

  10. The time the transfer takes effect, and who has been told (course addition).

The incoming leader’s job is to ask for what was not said. The most dangerous missing item is usually a restriction that the outgoing leader knows and did not write down.

Worked example

Outgoing ATL: “Perimeter is set, CNT has been trying the phone, command wants us to hold.” Incoming ATL asks: “What has command prohibited? What have you asked for that hasn’t arrived? What decision is waiting on someone? Who knows I’m taking over, and from what time?” The answers reveal that the commander has prohibited any approach to the east side because a gas meter was damaged. That was not in the log.

Common error

An implicit transfer. The outgoing leader drifts away, the incoming leader starts giving direction, and no one announces a time. For a period, two people or no one is in command.

3. Three message formats

Purpose Format Example (fictional)
Recommendation Situation; why it matters; options; recommendation; authorization needed “CNT reports the subject asked for his sister. She is at the command post. Contact through CNT may open dialogue; direct contact carries risk. Options: recorded message, phone through CNT, or decline. I recommend a recorded message reviewed by CNT. That needs your approval.”
Assignment Who; objective; constraints; report-back “Team 2: maintain observation of the north side. Do not move east of the fence line. Report any movement at once and a status every 20 minutes.”
Update What changed; effect on the mission; decision required “Update: EMS now reports 25 minutes to the nearest trauma center, up from 10, because of road ice. That lengthens the time to care for any casualty. Decision needed: whether to stage a second ambulance.”

An assignment without a constraint and a report-back is incomplete. An update that does not state the effect on the mission is only noise.

4. Role of the ATL

  • Track assignments and resources in the common operating picture.

  • Resolve conflicts in information before they reach the TL as two different stories.

  • Anticipate requests: relief, food, batteries, lighting, warming.

  • Confirm that key messages reached the right commander and were acknowledged.

  • Never give direction that conflicts with the TL’s. If you disagree, tell the TL.

5. Coordination

For each unit at the scene (patrol, dispatch, CNT, technical support, K9, medical, investigators), write the relationship:

  • Command: you or your chain can direct them.

  • Support: they provide a service to the operation under their own supervisor.

  • Liaison: you exchange information; neither directs the other.

Do not assume a message reached a unit because it was transmitted. A message is delivered when it is acknowledged.

6. Cognitive load

Module 2 explained that stress narrows attention. The leader controls this with structure:

  • Sort incoming information by its effect on safety and on the next decision. Everything else waits.

  • Delegate routine tracking to the ATL or a scribe, where policy allows.

  • Use the three formats and require read-backs for critical messages.

  • Use plain words. “East side, ground floor, second window from the north” is better than a private label.

  • On long incidents, plan relief for leaders as well as operators. A leader 14 hours into an incident is making worse decisions and is the last person to notice.

Lesson

1. Classify by facts

Five problem types need different information and have different objectives.

Type Defining facts First information need
Barricaded subject, alone Subject contained; no one else exposed inside Is anyone else inside? What is the subject’s state and capability?
Hostage A person is held and threatened Who, where, condition, and what the subject has said and done
Active threat Harm to people is occurring now Where the harm is occurring
High-risk planned arrest Legal process exists; time and place can be chosen Currency of the intelligence; who else will be present
Behavioral crisis Person in crisis; may or may not be armed; often no crime What has been said; history; who the person will talk to; medical needs

The presence of SWAT does not classify an incident. The label from dispatch does not classify it. A stated fact does: “Classified as possible hostage because the caller, who was inside until 1850, reports a second adult who has not come out.”

Common error

Keeping a classification because resources were called on the strength of it.

2. Escalation and de-escalation criteria

Four kinds of change matter:

  • Behavior: what the subject is doing or saying now compared with before.

  • Exposure: who is in danger now who was not, or who is no longer.

  • Objective: whether the incident commander’s objective has changed.

  • Time: whether time is now working for or against safety.

Grade every threat report three ways: credible, uncorroborated or stale. “He said he’d shoot anyone who comes in” heard by a negotiator five minutes ago is different from the same words reported third-hand from last week.

3. Working with negotiators

Negotiation and tactical planning serve the same objective. They fail each other when they do not share information. Agree on five things with the CNT leader early:

  1. The immediate safety priorities.

  2. What CNT needs the tactical team to avoid (for example, visible movement during a call).

  3. What tactical needs from CNT (subject’s location, mood, statements about others inside, deadlines).

  4. Any time limits or deadlines the subject has set.

  5. Who decides what, and how each side tells the other before acting.

Worked example

CNT learns that the subject becomes agitated when he sees officers near his truck. That is intelligence for the perimeter. The perimeter team sees a second light come on upstairs. That is intelligence for CNT. Neither helps unless there is a named person and a set time for passing it.

4. Medical considerations

At the leadership level, confirm five things and write them in the common operating picture: where medical support is staged; the route in and out; the transport time to the right level of care under present road conditions; known medical needs of the subject, victims or bystanders; and who the TEMS lead is. Medical tactics belong to qualified TEMS personnel. The leader’s job is to make sure the plan accounts for them.

5. Layered contingencies and worked triggers

For each strategy, ask what would make it unsuitable and what replaces it. A trigger is not “it has been four hours” and not “we are getting nowhere”. Elapsed time and frustration describe the team, not the subject or the risk.

Worked trigger 1 (fictional)

Strategy: hold and negotiate. Trigger: “CNT or any perimeter position reports a sound or statement indicating that a person inside is injured.” Response within authority: “ATL notifies TL at once; TL recommends to the tactical commander that the incident be reclassified and options reviewed.” Owner: TL.

Worked trigger 2 (fictional)

Strategy: hold a perimeter in winter conditions. Trigger: “Any perimeter position reports that it cannot be relieved within the exposure limit set at the brief.” Response: “ATL reports to TL with a recommendation to reduce positions, rotate from another element or request more personnel.” Owner: ATL.

Each trigger names an observable event, a response that fits the owner’s authority, and an owner.

6. The decision to hold

Holding is often the most defensible choice. It is a deliberate decision only when three conditions are met:

  1. The risk is being monitored. Someone is watching for each trigger.

  2. Resources are adequate. Relief, medical support and communications can be sustained.

  3. The next decision is scheduled. A time or an event is set for review.

If any one is missing, the team is not holding. It is waiting.

  1. A barricaded subject has been silent for three hours. A team member says, “No activity means low risk.” Give two reasons that statement may be wrong.

    Keyed answer

    Any two: silence gives no information about intent or condition; the subject may be injured, preparing or asleep; quiet can precede action; “no activity” is not a monitored trigger.

  2. Which of these is a usable trigger, and why are the others not? (a) “If negotiations stall.” (b) “If the subject is seen at the front window holding what an observer identifies as a long gun.” (c) “After six hours.”

    Keyed answer

    Answers: (b) is usable: specific, observable, communicable. (a) describes frustration. (c) is elapsed time.

  3. CNT tells you the subject has asked what time it gets dark. State who needs that information and why.

    Keyed answer

    The TL, tactical commander and perimeter positions, through the agreed CNT-tactical information channel. It may indicate a plan connected with darkness. It is intelligence, not a conclusion.

2. Diagnose the cause

A performance gap has one or more of seven causes. Each has a different fix.

Cause Evidence that points to it Fix
Knowledge gap Cannot state what is required Teach
Skill gap Knows what to do, cannot do it reliably Practice with feedback
Unclear expectation Different people describe the standard differently State and publish the standard
Equipment Task fails when equipment is involved Repair, replace or change the equipment
Coordination Each person performs; the handoff between them fails Change the procedure; practice the handoff
Workload Performs when fresh, fails when loaded or fatigued Reassign or reduce tasks
Deliberate non-compliance Knows, can, and chooses not to Supervisory and accountability process, not training
Common error

Prescribing training for every gap. Training fixes the first two causes only. If the cause is a procedure that drops messages at shift change, more training produces well-trained people using a procedure that drops messages.

3. How adults build skill

A short explanation, an attempt, immediate feedback, a corrected repetition and a later retest usually build more than a long lecture with no test. Two points drive this course’s own design: retrieving information from memory strengthens it more than rereading, and practice spread over time lasts longer than the same practice in one block. These are widely reported findings in learning research.

In practice: keep blocks short; make people perform, not listen; retest after a gap of days or weeks; and rotate the leader role in exercises so that more than one person practices it.

4. Psychological safety and accountability

These are two different things, and a team needs both.

  • Psychological safety means a team member can report an error, ask a question or challenge a plan without being ridiculed. Without it, the leader stops hearing bad news.

  • Accountability means reckless, dishonest or knowingly unsafe conduct has consequences through the proper process.

The line between them: an honest error reported promptly is a learning event. A concealed error or a knowing violation is a conduct matter.

How to give feedback

State the behavior you observed, its effect and the change you want, with a measure. “On the second run you gave the assignment without a report-back. The team did not report the door, and I learned of it four minutes late. Next run, every assignment ends with a report-back, and I will count them.”

5. Succession

A team with one capable leader has no depth. Assign planning, briefing, facilitation and debriefing tasks to others, within what policy allows. Assess what they demonstrate, not seniority or confidence.

2. The federal standard for force

Graham v. Connor held that a claim that officers used excessive force in an arrest, stop or other seizure is judged under the Fourth Amendment’s objective-reasonableness standard. The Court directed attention to the facts of each case, including three factors: the severity of the crime; whether the suspect poses an immediate threat to the safety of officers or others; and whether the suspect is actively resisting or attempting to evade arrest by flight. Reasonableness is judged from the perspective of a reasonable officer on the scene, not with hindsight, and the test is objective: the officer’s intent does not decide it.

Tennessee v. Garner held that deadly force may not be used to prevent the escape of an apparently unarmed, non-dangerous fleeing suspect. It is permitted to prevent escape only where the officer has probable cause to believe the suspect poses a significant threat of death or serious physical injury to the officer or others. Do not stretch Garner beyond its facts.

Barnes v. Felix (2025) rejected the “moment-of-threat” rule, under which a court looked only at the instant of the threat that led to the shooting. The Court held that the reasonableness inquiry covers the totality of the circumstances and has no time limit, so earlier events can bear on how a reasonable officer would understand later ones. The Court did not decide whether or how an officer’s own conduct in creating a dangerous situation affects the analysis. Barnes is therefore not a “provocation” or automatic-negligence rule.

What Barnes means for a leader (course commentary; confirm with counsel)

Planning, briefing and the decisions made before contact are part of the circumstances that may be examined. This is a further reason for the decision record in Module 2.

3. Avoid simple rules

The presence of a weapon or a serious offense does not, by itself, answer whether force is justified. Note the source of each word you use. “Immediate threat” is from Graham. Words such as “necessity” and “proportionality” appear in many agency policies and some state laws; they are not Graham’s listed factors. Use the terms APD policy uses, and cite the policy section.

Common error

Stating a professional standard as law (“NTOA requires…”). NTOA recommends. Policy and law require.

4. Supervisory exposure

After an incident, four separate questions can be asked: Was the individual’s use of force lawful and within policy? Was the planning and supervision reasonable? Was the training adequate? Was policy followed? A leader is most directly concerned with the second and third. The records that answer them are the brief, the risk register, the decision records and the training file. Document what was communicated, known, authorized and reassessed.

6. AAR lanes

An AAR is for learning. A criminal investigation decides whether a crime occurred. An administrative investigation decides whether policy was violated. They have different purposes, rules and consequences. Before an AAR on any incident that is or may be under investigation, ask the responsible official and counsel whether and when the AAR may proceed and how its records are handled. This paragraph is course guidance; cite APD policy in the local annex.

7. AAR procedure

The National Police Foundation guide sets out eight steps (pp. 33–51):

  1. Determine the type of AAR to conduct.

  2. Select a lead or team.

  3. Research the incident, compile materials and review information.

  4. Identify and engage key stakeholders.

  5. Conduct analysis.

  6. Develop findings and recommendations, or promising practices and lessons learned.

  7. Communicate findings.

  8. Implement lessons learned and recommendations.

For a team-level AAR, this course adapts those steps into a working sequence. The adaptation is course material.

  1. Set the scope: which incident, which questions, who attends.

  2. Build the chronology from records, with a source for every entry.

  3. Collect each participant’s view of what was known at each point.

  4. Mark the decision points.

  5. Compare what was expected with what happened.

  6. Find the contributing causes, using the seven categories from Module 6.

  7. Write each corrective action with an owner, a date and a verification method.

  8. Check later that each action was done and that it worked.

8. Hindsight controls

  • Judge each decision by what could reasonably be known at that time.

  • Ask “What did you know then?” before “What should you have done?”

  • Record what worked and why, as well as what failed. A good practice that is not recorded is lost.

  • A good outcome does not prove a sound process. Look for the near miss inside the success.

Worked example

Finding, weak: “Communication was poor.” Finding, strong: “At 0214 Team 2 transmitted a civilian-proximity warning. No acknowledgment was recorded. The warning reached the TL at 0221 (source: radio log, TL statement). Contributing cause: no acknowledgment requirement for priority transmissions during shift handoff (coordination). Corrective action: add a mandatory read-back for priority transmissions to the team procedure. Owner: ATL. Date: 30 days. Verification: observed in the next two exercises.”

Lesson

1. Integration

The capstone has no new content. It asks for every product at once, in sequence, with changing information. Before you start, write from memory: the four decision-rights categories; the CDM steps; the briefing order; the three formats; the hold conditions. If you cannot, review before you begin.

2. Command readiness

Readiness shows in consistent decisions and open escalation, not in the absence of doubt. A competent leader says, “I don’t know that yet; here is how I will find out,” and “That decision is above my authority; here is my recommendation.”

3. Portfolio defense

For any product in your portfolio you should be able to answer four questions: Why did you frame the situation that way? What alternative did you consider? What evidence did you use? What would have changed your decision? Write the answers for your capstone decision record.

4. Development continuity

A score is not approval. Use it to choose what to practice under supervision.

How to work a case

All cases are fictional. Names, places, times and unit designations are invented. The cases omit operational assault detail on purpose. They test classification, command, communication, planning, risk management and reassessment.

How to work a case. Read the packet. Complete the products the module asks for. Only then open the first inject in Part VIII. Complete the next product before you open the next inject. Read the model reasoning in Part IX last. No case has a single required answer.

Capstone · Northern Lights

Each entry shows one defensible line of reasoning. Other answers can meet the same criteria. Each lists “markers of a weak answer” so that you can find your own errors.

Initial record. Facts: a phone threat with a claim of a gun and a claim of being “at the building” (Ms. L; she did not see him). His vehicle is not nearby (patrol). No disturbance heard (patrol). A handgun purchase record exists. Unknowns: where Mr. A is; whether he is in the building; which apartments are occupied; the 2C/3C address conflict. Classification: threat complaint with a possible armed subject at an unconfirmed location; not a barricade or hostage incident on present facts. Exposed: about 25 people in the restaurant; residents of 16 apartments; Ms. L is safe. Time: snow is coming; the restaurant closes at 2200.

Brief and register. Risks to include: subject in the building, undetected; subject not in the building (resources committed in the wrong place); restaurant patrons and residents unaware; the address conflict; weather effects on medical and vehicle movement; single stairwell and elevator; information released to the public causing movement. Each with an owner and trigger. Authority: the tactical commander approves; the incident commander owns evacuation and notification decisions.

Inject 1. The address conflict is resolved (3C), and a new exposed person appears: an 81-year-old who lives alone in 2C and is not answering. Officers’ attention has been on the wrong apartment. Update all elements with read-back. Mr. H is a welfare concern in his own right.

Inject 2. Medical time is longer and a key vehicle is delayed: revise the register and tell the TL which parts of the plan depended on them. CNT’s contact supplies two things: Mr. A answers his phone, and road noise suggests he may be in a vehicle. That is evidence against “inside the building”. It is one observation; do not treat it as proof. Approvals needed: any change of mission or posture is the commander’s.

Inject 3. Mr. A is in custody 11 miles away. The reason for a tactical deployment at the building has largely ended. The decision record should ask: what purpose does continued deployment serve? Remaining tasks: confirm that no threat remains at the building (his statement is unverified; was anyone else involved?); locate the handgun, which is an investigative matter; and check on Mr. H, a welfare check that is still open. Defensible recommendation: stand down the tactical posture on the commander’s decision; hand the welfare check and the investigation to patrol and detectives; account for personnel and equipment; record the times.

Rejected alternative (example). Entering to clear the residential floors before the tenant list arrived: rejected because the subject’s presence was unverified, the address was in conflict, and the action could not be reversed.

AAR of your own work. Look for: the point at which you first wrote “not confirmed in the building”; whether you carried the 2C record after it was contradicted; whether Mr. H appears in your final recommendation.

Markers of a weak answer

Treats “I’m at the building” as a fact. No plan for the restaurant. Carries on with the deployment after inject 3 without stating a purpose. Forgets Mr. H.

Part IV

Final Knowledge Assessment

Take this after Module 7 and before the capstone. Closed notes. Write every answer before you open Part IX. One point each; partial credit is not given. Pass: 16 of 20. For each missed item, write one sentence that states why the keyed answer is correct. Items 1 to 12 are short scenarios. Items 13 to 20 are direct questions.

Scenario items

  1. You are ATL at a barricade. Your TL is on the phone with the commander. A perimeter position asks you for permission to move to a new position outside the area set in the brief. Your delegation covers “relief rotation within briefed positions”. What do you do, and which decision-rights category applies?

    Keyed answer

    Do not approve it. It is outside your delegation. Category B (TL approval): pass the request to the TL with your recommendation. If the reason is an immediate safety need, category D may apply as policy defines it, with immediate notification. Why: authority follows the delegation, not availability.

  2. Dispatch labels a call “hostage”. The only basis is a neighbor who “thinks someone else is in there”. Write the classification sentence you would put in your decision record.

    Keyed answer

    Example: “Possible hostage, unverified: one neighbor believes a second person may be inside; no direct observation.” Any answer that states the claim, its single source and its unverified status. Wrong: “Hostage situation.”

  3. Ninety minutes into a quiet barricade, a supervisor says, “We’ve waited long enough.” Which bias is the risk here, and what question do you ask first?

    Keyed answer

    Action bias. First question: what is time doing to the risk, and has any trigger occurred?

  4. A detective tells you firearms are “definitely” at the address. The source is an associate’s comment of unknown date. How do you record this in the threat table?

    Keyed answer

    As unverified, low confidence, single source, undated, with the source described. Not as a fact.

  5. During a planned-arrest brief you ask two operators to state the end state. Their answers differ. What does this tell you, and what do you do?

    Keyed answer

    The brief failed its test. Restate the end state, ask for read-back again, and do not proceed until it is consistent.

  6. You relieve an ATL whose brief ends, “Nothing pending.” The log shows a request to the commander 15 minutes ago with no answer recorded. What do you ask?

    Keyed answer

    “What was requested, of whom, and has there been an answer?” Then record it as pending with a time.

  7. Write a complete assignment message for this intent: Team 2 should watch the rear door.

    Keyed answer

    Must contain who, objective, constraint and report-back. Example: “Team 2: observe the rear door. Remain at the north corner. Report any movement at once and status every 15 minutes.”

  8. EMS tells you road ice has doubled the transport time to the hospital. Write the update to your TL in the update format.

    Keyed answer

    Must contain what changed, the effect and the decision required. Example: “Update: EMS transport time is now 40 minutes, up from 20, because of ice. Any casualty waits twice as long for hospital care. Decision needed: request a second ambulance or a closer staging point?”

  9. CNT learns the subject can see officers from an upstairs window and is angered by it. Who needs this information, and what is the mechanism that makes sure they get it?

    Keyed answer

    The TL and the positions that can be seen from the window. Mechanism: the agreed CNT-tactical liaison (a named person and a set reporting time), recorded in the common operating picture.

  10. At a behavioral-crisis call, a person holding a knife is alone in an empty lot and not advancing. The store nearby opens in 20 minutes. What has time just become, and what do you recommend the incident commander consider?

    Keyed answer

    Time is now working against safety because exposure will rise at opening. Recommend the incident commander consider controlling exposure first (for example, delaying the opening or keeping people away from the lot) so that time can continue to work for a peaceful resolution. Any answer that addresses exposure before speed is acceptable. Wrong: “Resolve it before the store opens.”

  11. Your team has missed radio acknowledgments in two exercises. A colleague proposes a four-hour radio class. What do you do before you agree?

    Keyed answer

    Diagnose the cause first. Find out whether the gap is knowledge, expectation, coordination, workload or equipment. A class fixes only a knowledge or skill gap.

  12. An operation ended with an arrest and no injuries. The radio log shows a warning that no one acknowledged for seven minutes. A team member says, “It worked, so the plan was fine.” Name the bias and state the finding you would write.

    Keyed answer

    Outcome bias. Finding example: “A priority warning went unacknowledged for seven minutes (radio log). The arrest element could have moved during that time.”

Direct items

  1. What does TROS say about whether agencies must comply with it?

    Keyed answer

    NTOA does not mandate compliance; agencies that use the standard do so voluntarily (TROS p. 8).

  2. List the four NTOA safety priorities in order.

    Keyed answer

    Hostages/victims; innocent bystanders/the public; public safety personnel (including SWAT and EMS); hostage taker/suspect (TROS p. 11).

  3. List the five steps of the PERF Critical Decision-Making Model.

    Keyed answer

    Gather (collect) information; assess situation, threats and risks; consider police powers and agency policy; identify options and determine the best course of action; act, review and reassess.

  4. State the three factors named in Graham v. Connor.

    Keyed answer

    Severity of the crime; whether the suspect poses an immediate threat to the safety of officers or others; whether the suspect is actively resisting or attempting to evade arrest by flight.

  5. What did Barnes v. Felix reject, and what question did it leave undecided?

    Keyed answer

    It rejected the moment-of-threat rule. It left undecided whether or how an officer’s creation of a dangerous situation bears on reasonableness.

  6. Give the three properties of a usable decision trigger.

    Keyed answer

    Specific, observable, communicable.

  7. Name the three conditions under which holding is a deliberate decision.

    Keyed answer

    Risk is monitored; resources are adequate; the next decision is scheduled.

  8. State the difference between an AAR and an administrative investigation.

    Keyed answer

    An AAR examines an incident to learn and improve. An administrative investigation determines whether policy was violated. They have different purposes, rules and consequences.

Example 1 — Completed decision record

Fictional. Based on this report: “1910 hours. Caller says her neighbor, Mr. E, is ‘holding his wife at gunpoint’. Patrol arrives 1918 and hears nothing. Mrs. E’s car is not in the driveway.”

Field Entry
Date, time, your role (date), 1925, on-call ATL, phone consultation
Facts Caller reported at 1910 that Mr. E is holding his wife at gunpoint (source: 911 caller; she has not said what she saw). Patrol on scene 1918: no sound from the house (patrol). Mrs. E’s car is not in the driveway (patrol, 1918).
Inferences A hostage may be present. Or Mrs. E may not be home.
Unknowns What the caller saw or heard herself. Where Mrs. E is. Whether Mr. E is home. Whether a firearm is in the house. Patrol sergeant is re-contacting the caller; dispatch is calling Mrs. E’s phone.
Classification and fact behind it Unverified hostage report. One source, basis unknown. I will treat a hostage as possible and not as established. Evidence that would disprove it: Mrs. E reached elsewhere.
Safety objective and persons exposed Protect any person inside; protect neighbors and officers. Exposed: Mrs. E (if inside), adjacent homes, patrol.
Decision required now Whether to recommend that the TL request a call-out, and what patrol should do in the next ten minutes.
Authority check Call-out is a commander decision (C). I recommend to the TL. Advice to patrol is advice; the patrol sergeant commands the scene.
Options and cost (1) Recommend call-out now: resources moving early; cost is a response to an unverified report. (2) Wait for verification: saves resources; cost is delay if the report is true. (3) Recommend call-out and verification in parallel.
Recommendation and why Option 3. If the report is true, the first safety priority is engaged and time matters. Verification takes minutes and can cancel the call-out.
Communicated To TL by phone 1927, acknowledged. To patrol sergeant 1926: suggested caller re-interview and a call to Mrs. E’s phone; acknowledged.
Trigger and owner If Mrs. E is reached and is safe elsewhere: I notify TL at once and recommend reclassification to possible barricade or welfare check. Owner: me.

Example 2 — Completed strategic brief (extract)

Fictional planned arrest of “Mr. Q” on a felony warrant. This is a different case from Case D. It shows the level of a strategic brief: no positions, no methods.

Section Entry
1. Situation Mr. Q is wanted on a felony warrant for assault with a weapon. He works day shifts at a warehouse and lives with his mother in a single-family house.
2. Verified / unverified Verified: warrant (copy attached, checked today). Work schedule (employer, yesterday). Unverified: “He carries a knife” (co-worker, undated). Whether his mother is at home during the day (unknown).
3. Mission, purpose, end state Take Mr. Q into custody on the warrant. Purpose: investigators assess the arrest as beyond patrol capability because of the charged offense. End state: Mr. Q in custody; no injuries; no uninvolved person exposed; scene released to investigators.
4. Authority and constraints Arrest warrant. Approved by tactical commander (name, date). Constraint: no entry into the residence is planned; any change requires commander approval and legal review.
5. Assignments Arrest element: TL. Observation: Team 2 leader. Investigators: Detective (name), stand by to take custody.
6. Communications TAC-1. Priority transmissions are read back. Status every 15 minutes.
7. Medical and logistics Ambulance staged at (location); 8 minutes to trauma center; TEMS lead (name).
8. Contingencies See risk register (Example 3).
9. Read-back Team 2 leader read back the end state and trigger 2. Detective read back the custody handoff.

Example 5 — A weak answer and a strong answer

Case: Example 1 above. Task: recommendation to the TL, 100 words or fewer.

Weak

“We have a hostage situation with an armed suspect. Recommend full call-out and prepare for rescue. Patrol should hold the perimeter until we get there.”

Why it is weak: it states an unverified report as fact; it names no unknowns; it has no authority check; it names a tactic before an objective; it has no trigger.

Strong

“One caller reports Mr. E holding his wife at gunpoint; we don’t yet know what she saw. Patrol hears nothing and Mrs. E’s car is gone. I’m treating a hostage as possible, not confirmed. Patrol is re-interviewing the caller and dispatch is trying Mrs. E’s phone. Recommend you ask the commander for a call-out now; we can cancel if she’s located. If she is reached and safe, I’ll call you at once and recommend we reclassify.”

Why it is strong: facts are separated from the report; unknowns have owners; the recommendation fits the ATL’s authority; the trigger is specific and owned.
